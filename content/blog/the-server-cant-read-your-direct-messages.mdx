Direct messages landed in 1.7 without encryption. There was a line above every
conversation saying so, that this one wasn't private and the server could read it.
Honest, and also a to-do list.

Two weeks later the line was gone. A direct message is sealed on your machine before
it goes anywhere, and the server stores something it can't open. You don't switch it
on and you can't forget to.

What I keep thinking about is the two separate times the whole feature was
switched off and nothing anywhere said so.

## What sealing is

Every message gets its own key. That key is wrapped once for each person in the
conversation, using the key they published, and the wrapped copies ride along with
the ciphertext. The server takes the whole thing and files it. It has no key to any
of it.

Attachments go the same way. They used to go up as themselves, get a thumbnail made,
and be served to anybody holding the link, so a private conversation could have
public photographs in it. Now the file is encrypted before upload and its key sits
inside the sealed message.

It only seals when everybody in the conversation has a key it's willing to use.
Sealing for four people out of five leaves the fifth looking at a conversation they
can't read while everyone else sees it working, which is worse than not sealing at
all. When it can't seal, the line above the messages comes back and says why.

## Half of every signature would have been rejected

The desktop did its crypto with WebCrypto and the phone couldn't. `crypto.subtle`
isn't on React Native, so mobile was already doing its curve work with
`@noble/curves`. Two implementations of one envelope is a pair of clients that send
each other messages nobody can read, with the sender looking at the text they typed
either way. So the desktop moved to noble too.

Nothing on the wire changed. Then the test vectors failed.

ECDSA has two valid signatures for every message: `s`, and `order - s`. Noble refuses
the high one by default. That's the right default for a blockchain, where a signature
you can rewrite and keep valid means a transaction you can replay under a second id.
Nothing here is identified by its signature.

WebCrypto doesn't normalise, and neither does JOSE. So about **half of all ES256
signatures come out high**. With noble's default left on, roughly half of every
client's published keys would have been rejected at random with "the signature does
not check out", and the other half would have worked perfectly.

That's the shape of a bug that survives a lot of testing and then turns up months
later as "encryption sometimes doesn't work". It only surfaced because the test
vector happened to have a high `s`, which was luck. The check signs 24 of them now
and fails unless at least one comes out high, so a future run can't pass on a lucky
sample.

## Twice it was off, and nothing said so

**Nobody who was already a member ever published a key.** The publish ran from the
`server:joined` handler. A client that already holds a token never goes near that.
It sends `session:restore` on connect instead, and none of what that does is
`server:joined`. So encryption worked for anybody who joined a server after the
feature shipped, and for nobody who was already in one. No key left the device,
nothing errored, and the composer correctly reported that the other side hadn't
published anything. It was right. The server really was saying that.

**And an account on two devices had it switched off.** A peer pins two things: your
message key, and the thumbprint of whatever vouched for it. The message key comes
from your seed, so every device agrees on it. The signing key didn't. For an account
it was generated fresh and randomly on each device, so a laptop and a phone signed
into one account published two different vouchers for the same key. Whichever spoke
last was what the server held. Every peer watched the thumbprint flip, and a flipping
thumbprint is what a substituted key looks like, so their clients refused to encrypt.
Correctly. The people most likely to own two devices were the ones it was quietly off
for.

There was a check meant to catch the first of those, and it asserted that both
handlers *called* the publish function. Asserting a call reaches a function isn't
asserting that the function does the thing. It reads the function now, and fails on
any early return before the key goes out.

## What a pin proves, and what it doesn't

Pinning a key catches one that changed. It can't tell you the first one was ever
right, because the server is what introduced the two of you, and no check made
through the server gets past that.

The only thing that does is two people reading a short code to each other over
something the server isn't on. A phone call, or standing in the same room.

It's built from everything each side has pinned about the other: both thumbprints and
both message keys. Substitute any one of the four and the two codes differ, and the
server can't do anything about that, because it never sees the comparison. The four
are sorted before hashing so both people compute the same string without having to
agree who goes first. Two honest people reading out different codes would decide they
were being attacked, which is worse than no check at all.

It's digits rather than words. There are already 24 words on the identity backup
card, and eight more words next to them would read as a second recovery phrase.
Something to keep secret and type into a box, which is the exact opposite of what
this is.

The button says "read this to them", and afterwards the card says "compared and
matched". It says what happened rather than implying the card did anything. The mark
goes the moment either key moves, because somebody who compared a code last year, and
whose peer has since arrived with a new key, has verified nothing.

## The warning that cried wolf

For a while, joining a server would tell you it was showing a message key that wasn't
yours, and that you should treat your direct messages as readable by it.

It fired on a race. We publish our own key on join, and the first member list usually
arrives before that lands, so the mismatch was genuinely there for about a second. It
also never went away, because it had no dismiss and no expiry, so a one-second flash
stayed on screen for the rest of the session. And it blamed the wrong thing. The
usual cause wasn't a hostile server. It was signing in on a second device.

It waits five seconds now, comes down the moment the keys agree, and names the second
device first. That isn't a fix for the cause. The cause was that a second device made
a whole new identity, and that's what the message password in
[1.9](/changelog/1.9.0) is for.

## What this doesn't cover

Channels aren't encrypted, and aren't pretending to be. A direct message is between
people who chose each other. A channel is a room with a member list somebody
administers. People join it, leave it and get removed from it, and the server has to
hand the history to whoever arrived yesterday.

So if you're hosting, the honest version is this. Your server can read what's said in
its channels. It can't read a direct message between two of its members.
