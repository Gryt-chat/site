**Title:** One key for all your devices\
**Date:** 26 October 2026\
**Author:** Sivert Gullberg Hansen\
**Keywords:** end-to-end encryption, MLS, device linking, key handover, iCloud Keychain

## Abstract

Direct messages in Gryt have been end-to-end encrypted since September, and since
1.12 they run on MLS, so every message gets its own key. That part held up. The
trouble was everything around it: how a new phone or computer gets your key, what
happens when it can't, and who's allowed to change it. Each device quietly made a
key of its own, so one account could turn into three people. A password-locked copy
of your key sat on the server, where anyone with the database could keep guessing.
And one account could have a different key on each server. This post goes through
what broke, what I tried, and the model Gryt uses now: one key per account, new
devices approved by one you already have, and only your own devices can ever change
that key. Nobody has to remember a password for it. It also shows the sketches I
drew with Claude while working it out, because the screens are where most of the
mistakes were.

## 1 Introduction

I've been working on encrypted DMs for a few months now, and for a while it felt
like every fix turned up the next problem. My first plan worked when I tested it on
one computer. It stopped working the moment I used Gryt the way people actually do:
a desktop, a phone, a browser at work, a new laptop.

None of that time was wasted. Every one of those problems told me something about
what the design had to guarantee, and the version in beta now is built on them.

## 2 Where it started

DMs shipped unencrypted in 1.7, with a line in every conversation saying the server
could read them. Two weeks later they were encrypted on your machine [1]. That first
design used one key made from your 24 words, for everything, forever. If that key
ever leaked, every DM the server had stored could be opened.

1.12 moved DMs to MLS [2], the IETF standard for group messaging. Every message gets
its own key, deleted once it's been decrypted, so a key stolen later can't open old
messages. MLS also handles adding and removing devices, which is the part this post
is about.

## 3 What went wrong

### 3.1 Every device made its own key

In MLS each device is a member of the conversation. Gryt then needs to know which
devices belong to which person. Early on, every device simply made its own identity
the first time it needed one. So my desktop, my phone and my browser were three
different "me"s, and the apps of the people I talked to had to decide which one to
trust.

It showed up as a bug report from myself. My desktop said a DM with Sable was
encrypted, but every message failed with "this device hasn't been added yet", and
her replies never arrived. Only a device that's already in a conversation can add
another one, and nothing was adding mine.

### 3.2 A password on the server

To get your key onto a new device without another device around, Gryt kept a copy
of it on your account, locked with a message password. It's a common approach, and
I talked myself into it. But it means the server holds something that unlocks your
account. Anyone who gets that database can try passwords for as long as they like,
and most people's passwords don't survive that. It's gone now, and devices that
still had a copy deleted it themselves.

### 3.3 A different key on each server

The last one I found while testing a fresh Mac in October. My account used my
iPhone's key on one server and my old Mac's key on another, left over from before
any of this was tidied up. The new Mac got approved on one and was stuck on the
other, and the only way out was a button called "Start fresh". Worse, that button
meant anyone signed in to my account could replace my key, and my contacts would
only see a warning.

## 4 How it works now

### 4.1 One key, many devices

Your account has one key. The first device you use makes it, and no other device
ever makes its own. Every other device gets that key from one you already have.

On an iPhone or a Mac, the key also rides along in iCloud Keychain, end to end
encrypted by Apple. On Android it goes in your Google backup. So a new Apple device
signed in to the same Apple ID often has your key before you've done anything.

### 4.2 New devices are approved

On a new computer, Gryt shows a code. You point your phone's camera at it, Gryt
opens and asks "Is this your Mac?", and both screens show the same four pictures.
If they match, you approve with Face ID, and your phone hands the key over, along
with your older messages.

![A sketch of the new-computer window: "Approve this computer from your iPhone", a QR code with "Point your iPhone's camera here", and buttons for "My iPhone isn't here" and "How does this work?". Underneath it says servers and channels work right away, and only DMs wait for this](/blog/one-key-for-all-your-devices/scene-new-computer.webp)

![A phone sketch: "Is this your Mac?", Gryt on macOS near Gjøvik, four emoji to compare, and "Yes, approve" or "No, they're different"](/blog/one-key-for-all-your-devices/phone-ask.webp)

The pictures are a check that you're approving the device in front of you, and not
one somebody else started. A request also reaches your phone as a notification, so
you don't have to go looking for it.

### 4.3 Only your key can change your key

This is the rule that fixed 3.3. A server now refuses to change the key your account
uses unless the current key signs off on it. When a server still has an older key
for you, the device that holds it gets one question, "Your iPhone's key will take
over on this server. Approve?", and signs a short note saying so. A note like that
can only come from one of your devices, or from your 24 words.

Someone who only has your password can't make one, so they can't swap your key on a
server you use. The worst they can do is sign in and ask, and your phone asks you
first.

### 4.4 When you've lost everything

If you've lost every device and your 24 words, there's still a way back, and it's
slow on purpose. You ask for a reset, and your new key only takes over after 30
days. Until then, every device of yours that's left is asked "Was this you?" and
can stop it, and the people you talk to see that your key is being reset. Thirty
days is long enough that a thief can't finish one while you're on holiday.

![A sketch of the "Lost your iPhone?" window, explaining what removing it does: it's signed out and taken out of every DM, your other devices carry on, and if you find it again you approve it like a new phone](/blog/one-key-for-all-your-devices/lost-phone.webp)

### 4.5 Messages that wait

One small thing that used to look broken: writing in a DM before your new device has
been added to it. It used to fail with "try again in a bit". Now the message waits,
says so, and sends itself once the device is in.

![A sketch of a DM with Pimby on a new Mac. The message "hey pimby" says "Sends when Pimby's app lets this Mac in", and a note explains the Mac is new to the chat and gets added by itself](/blog/one-key-for-all-your-devices/dm-waiting.webp)

## 5 The sketches

The pictures in this post are sketches I drew with Claude on a canvas, before
building anything, because the screens kept turning out to be where
the design was wrong. Writing "a device asks to be approved" is easy. Drawing the
window someone sees on a new computer at eleven at night makes you notice it says
"another device" when it should say "your iPhone", or that it asks a question
nobody can answer.

That's how Gryt uses AI in general [3]. Claude helped draft these screens and much
of the code behind them. Anything that touches your keys only merges after I've
read the whole diff myself.

## 6 What this doesn't protect against

- **Your Apple ID or Google account.** If someone has your Gryt password and your
  Apple ID, they get your key from iCloud Keychain. That's the same trade-off as
  Apple's own Messages, and your Apple ID is the lock there.
- **An unlocked phone in someone else's hand.** Approving asks for Face ID or your
  passcode, which helps, but it can't help if they have both.
- **Whoever runs the server.** They can change rows in their own database. Your
  contacts' apps still remember your old key and warn about the change, but they
  only warn. The real fix is a public, append-only log of account keys
  that every app checks, often called key transparency. That's next.
- **A server you've never joined.** Someone with your password can still join a
  server you're not on, as you. None of your contacts are there, and the key log
  above covers this too.

## 7 What's next

All of this is in the beta now: app 1.15.0-beta.10, server 1.12.2-beta.6 and the
latest phone builds. Once it's held up on real devices it goes to everyone. After
that, the key log from section 6, and a way to keep your history in a locked box on
a server you choose, so a new device can have everything even when your phone is
off.
