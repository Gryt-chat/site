**Title:** Your guest identity now fits in a password manager\
**Date:** 14 August 2026\
**Author:** Sivert Gullberg Hansen\
**Keywords:** identity, key derivation, recovery phrase, BIP 39, multi-device, privacy

## Abstract

Since 1.5.0 you haven't needed an account to use Gryt: your client makes a key, and
the key is who you are. But the key lived in one browser on one machine, and each
server got its own, so there was nothing single you could back up or carry to your
phone. Per-server keys are now derived from one 32-byte seed and the server's
identity. Any device with the seed arrives at the same key for any server, including
ones you join later, so there's nothing left to sync. Two servers still can't tell
they're talking to the same person. The seed is shown as 24 words with a built-in
checksum, made for pasting into a password manager. The same work fixed a bug where
a server that changed address handed you a different identity. Bans on servers that
accept guests are still easy to dodge, and this isn't federation.

## 1 Introduction

<Image src="/blog/recovery-key.webp" alt="Gryt's Security settings, with a warning that losing this device without saving your recovery key loses the roles, ownership and history on every server you joined without an account, above View recovery key and Restore identity buttons" width="2880" height="1514" />

**Figure 1:** The Security settings, with the warning about what losing this device
costs.

In 1.5.0 [1] you stopped needing an account to use Gryt. No email, no sign-up, no
round trip to anything I run. Your client makes a keypair, the key is who you are,
and that's enough. Joining a server only ever needed proof that the same person
came back, and signing a challenge proves that on its own.

I still think that was the right call. But it had a bad day built into it, and the
bad day looked like this: the key lived in one browser on one machine. Clear your
site data and it was gone. With it went every server that identity was known on:
your roles, your history, and any server you owned (Figure 1). Nobody could give it
back, including me, because there was nothing to give back. The key *was* the
account.

There was an escape hatch. You could save your identity to a file and restore it
somewhere else. It worked, and almost nobody used it, and honestly I understand
why: it went out of date every time you joined a new server. Each server got its
own key, so the file you exported last week didn't know about the server you
joined yesterday.

Carlo had been asking about federation, whether his users could join servers he
didn't run. That's a much bigger question and I'll come back to it another time.
Somewhere in the middle of it he asked something smaller:

> But if you allow multiple servers for one key for gryt one could store the key
> in a password manager for cross device sign in

Not "can I federate", just "can I be me on my phone". The answer was no because
there was no single thing to store. There were N things, one per server, and N kept
going up. So this post asks:

1. **How can one secret stand for every server** without making you trackable
   between them?
2. **What form should that secret take** so people actually keep it?

## 2 Design

### 2.1 One seed, a key per server

The keys aren't generated any more. They're calculated.

There's one seed on your device, 32 random bytes, and every server's key is worked
out from it, using the seed and that server's identity as the inputs. Same seed and
same server always produce the same key, on any device, whether or not that device
has ever spoken to that server before.

Your laptop and your phone don't have to exchange anything. Give them both the seed
and they independently arrive at the same key for any server, including one you join
next month. There's nothing left to sync, which means there's nothing left to go
stale.

The property I was most worried about losing survived intact. Two servers still
can't tell they're talking to the same person. The derived keys look unrelated to
anyone who doesn't hold the seed, so the per-server separation that guest mode was
built around is exactly as strong as it was.

### 2.2 Why the backup is words

The seed is shown as 24 words, taken directly from how Bitcoin wallets do recovery
phrases [2]. It's a good format and there was no reason to invent another. The
checksum is inside the words rather than sitting beside them. Table 1 shows how the
bits add up.

**Table 1:** How 24 words hold a 256-bit seed and its checksum.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Words in the standard list | 2048 |
| Bits per word | 11 |
| Bits in 24 words | 264 |
| Bits of seed | 256 |
| Bits of checksum, a hash of the seed | 8 |

So a mistyped or swapped word gets rejected instead of quietly making you somebody
else. That's the failure I actually cared about, because being silently wrong is much
worse than being told no. No two words in the list share their first four letters
either, so a misread word usually isn't a word at all and fails before the checksum
is even reached.

You're not meant to memorise them. Copy them into your password manager and move on
with your day. The field you paste them back into is a real password field, so your
manager will offer to save them the first time and fill them in after that. I use
Bitwarden, self-hosted via Vaultwarden [3], and this was very much designed around
that being the normal thing to do.

### 2.3 Using it

Don't have an account and want to be signed in on your phone?

1. On your computer: **Settings → You → Security → Show my identity backup**
2. Copy the 24 words
3. On your phone: **Settings → You → Security → I already have one**, and paste

Same person, same servers, same roles.

It's deliberately out of the way. A first-time user shouldn't have to think about
seeds, and nothing in the join flow mentions any of this. It's in settings under
**Security**, next to the identity tools that were already there, for people who go
looking.

## 3 Results: identities were filed under an address

While I was in here I found something I hadn't noticed. Your identity was filed under
the *address* a server answered on. Servers move. Gryt picks a different port on its
own if the one it wants is taken, and a self-hosted server on a home network gets a
new address whenever the router feels like it.

When that happened, the client recognised the server correctly, and then handed it a
completely different identity. You showed up as a stranger with no roles and no
history, and nothing on screen said why. It looked like the server had forgotten you.
That's also what broke direct messages on a second device.

Identities now belong to the server itself rather than to where it happens to be
reachable. So it can move, and it can rotate its own identity key, and you're still
you. A server you can reach both on your LAN and through a tunnel is now one identity
on both, where it used to be two.

## 4 Discussion: limitations

- **Bans on guests are easy to dodge.** Making a new identity is still free, and it
  always will be. If you host a server that accepts people without accounts, a ban
  holds only until somebody decides to come back as somebody else. That comes with
  not requiring an account. The answer is still invites, the lobby, or requiring
  accounts if it matters to you.
- **This isn't federation.** It makes you portable across your own devices. It doesn't
  make you portable across identity providers, and Carlo still can't bring his own
  users to a server I run. There's groundwork in this release that makes that safe to
  build later, but it's groundwork and nothing more.
- **The seed is everything.** Anyone with the 24 words is you, on every server.

## 5 Conclusion

**How can one secret stand for every server?** Derive each server's key from one
seed and that server's identity. Every device with the seed gets the same keys, and
the keys still look unrelated to the servers.

**What form should it take?** 24 words with a checksum built in, pasted into a
password manager rather than memorised.

Thanks to Carlo for the question. It was a better one than the one we had been
arguing about.
