Gryt's voice has always gone over WebRTC, like almost every voice app in a browser.
This month we finally got the courage to try the other thing: our own connection,
with our own Opus build and our own jitter buffer. In every test we've run it's been
faster. Over the internet it cut about 27 ms off a call.

It's experimental, but you can try it today. Beta builds use it by default, and the
stable app has a switch for it under Settings, General, Advanced. The voice server
has to offer it too, which it does from version 1.0.76. On an older one the switch
does nothing, and the call stays on WebRTC. A few things are still missing, and
they're listed further down.

## Why bother

My girlfriend and I use Gryt every day, on cables to the same router, and the voice
still felt a bit slow sometimes. So I wanted to know where the time actually went.

The test we ended up with is simple. The sender's microphone is swapped for a beep, a
1 kHz tone every three seconds. The listener notes when it hears each one. When both
run on the same machine they share a clock, so there's nothing to sync. We also
listen in at a few points along the way, so the total can be split into stages.

## Where the time went

On one machine, with nothing in between, Gryt's WebRTC path took about 50 to 64 ms
from mic to speaker. Some of that was ours to fix:

- eSports mode still ran the compressor, limiter and clipper. That's 6, 6 and 4 ms.
- eSports mode asked for 10 ms audio frames, but in the wrong half of the handshake,
  so Chrome kept using 20.
- Reconnecting reused the same audio track, and every reconnect left the call a bit
  slower until you left voice. That was the "it gets laggier the longer we talk"
  we'd noticed at home.
- Turning everything off now really takes it out of the chain. With no processing,
  the raw microphone goes straight out.

Those fixes shipped in betas earlier this month. But some of it wasn't ours to fix.
Chrome's own jitter buffer sat at about 30 ms whatever we asked for, and getting
audio into and out of WebRTC cost another 12 or so.

## What we built instead

Opus itself was never the slow part. It can work in 2.5 ms frames and costs a
fraction of a millisecond to encode. The waiting is in the buffers around it. So we
kept Opus and replaced everything around it:

- **Opus in WebAssembly**, built from the official libopus source, set to its
  lowest-delay mode with 2.5 ms frames.
- **An AudioWorklet** that encodes and decodes on the audio thread. The gate, volume,
  auto gain, compressor and limiter are plain code in there, with no lookahead.
- **WebTransport** to the voice server, with each packet sent as a datagram. Nothing
  gets resent, because a late packet is no use to anyone.
- **Our own jitter buffer**, sized from the jitter it actually sees. After a burst it
  drains back down instead of staying big.
- **The voice server** forwards each packet to everyone else in the room and puts
  the sender's id in front. It takes that id from the session, never from the
  packet.

In a browser without WebTransport, or on a network that blocks the UDP it needs, the
same packets go over a normal WebSocket to the voice server instead. It's slower on a
bad network, but you're still on the same voice as everyone else.

On the phone and in the Mac desktop app it goes one step further. A small engine in
C does the capture, the Opus and the playback itself, and talks to the voice server
over plain UDP. On a Mac, the round trip through the sound system alone went from
13.3 ms in Chrome to 3.3 ms in the engine. Video and screen sharing stay on WebRTC
everywhere. Only voice moved.

## The numbers

All medians, with the beep test above. Each run is a few minutes long.

| Where | WebRTC | Our connection |
|---|---|---|
| One machine, local voice server | 105 ms | 80 ms |
| Both clients on the Mac, voice server on another box on the LAN | 94 ms | 79 ms |
| Over the internet, out through our VPS and back | 102 ms | 75 ms |
| Two machines on one router, voice server in the house (round trip) | 174 ms | 133 ms |

The last row is a round trip. Two machines don't share a clock, so the second one
plays whatever it hears straight back, and the first times its own beep coming home.
That's the setup my girlfriend and I actually use. Over WebRTC the beep took 174 ms
there and back. Over our connection it took 133, or 121 once we asked the browser
for its smallest audio buffer. Call it 20 to 25 ms each way.

These totals include our own measuring, which costs 20 to 40 ms by itself: spotting a
beep means listening for a moment first. Both columns pay that, so the gap between
them is the real part. Counting only from when the beep leaves the sender to when
it's heard, the internet run was 79 ms on WebRTC and 52 on ours.

Over the internet, WebRTC's jitter buffer also kept stretching and squeezing the
audio to keep up. Ours didn't have to fill in a single gap in that run.

We checked the boring parts too. Mute let no beeps through, and push to talk only let
them through while the key was held. Switching microphones mid-call kept the voice
going. Killing the voice server mid-call brought both people back on the new
connection about 8 seconds later, with no extra delay afterwards.

## How it sounds, and what it costs

It sends 192 kbps, or 256 when your microphone really is stereo. Gryt's WebRTC voice
was 64 kbps mono by default. In a listening test with a song, 96 was the lowest that held up,
128 was great, and 192 sounded the same as the original. A recording of a real call
on the new connection came out at the original's level and very clear. A mono
microphone gains nothing from the stereo part.

The catch is data. To keep the delay down it sends 400 small packets a second, and
the headers on those cost about 190 kbps by themselves. So at the default you upload
about 380 kbps, which is a lot more than WebRTC used. That's why there's a voice
quality setting from 96 to 192 kbps, on the desktop under Advanced and on the phone
under Voice & video. Turn it down on phone data or weak Wi-Fi. If a channel has a max
bitrate, you send whichever is lower, but never below 96.

For a while your voice also went out over WebRTC as well, so people without the new
connection could still hear you. That meant uploading it twice. With the newest beta
app and server, the WebRTC copy only goes while someone in the call actually needs
it.

## What doesn't work yet

- **Older voice servers.** A voice server older than 1.0.76 doesn't offer the new
  connection, so calls there stay on WebRTC, like before.
- **Android.** The engine runs on the iPhone, in TestFlight builds for now. Android
  phones stay on WebRTC until it's built there too.
- **Mixed calls, one way.** Someone on an older app version can't hear people on the
  phone or the Mac engine, because those don't send a WebRTC copy at all.
- **The Mac App Store version** can't ship the C engine, so it uses the browser
  version of the new connection.
- **Lost packets.** A packet that doesn't arrive is filled in by Opus, which you can
  hear on a really bad connection. We want each packet to carry a small copy of the
  one before it, so one lost packet can be rebuilt from the next without adding
  delay.
- **Self-hosting.** The voice server needs one more UDP port open, 4433 by default.
  If it's closed, voice goes over the WebSocket instead.

## Is it safe

It's a new public endpoint, so we treated it like one. In the browser, audio is
encrypted with TLS 1.3 as part of QUIC, the same way WebRTC encrypts it with DTLS. The
voice server's certificate is short-lived and pinned: the app gets its fingerprint
over the connection it already trusts, so it can't be swapped in between. The C
engine's UDP is sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 under a key that comes over that same
trusted connection, and anything replayed is thrown away.

To get in at all you need a one-time ticket, which the voice server only hands out
after you've joined the room. It's gone after 30 seconds or one use. The voice server
checks the size of every packet, caps how fast one person can send, and never takes
the sender's id from the packet.

Still on the list before it's the default: fuzzing the packet path, and a cap on
sessions per IP address.

## What's next

The plan is to drop WebRTC voice entirely. The new connection becomes the default for
everyone, then the WebRTC microphone goes. Before that, Android needs the engine, and the
lost-packet work above needs doing. If you're on the beta, or you've flipped the
switch, you're already using it, and we'd love to hear how it sounds.
