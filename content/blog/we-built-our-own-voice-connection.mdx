**Title:** We built our own voice connection: replacing WebRTC voice in Gryt\
**Date:** 23 October 2026\
**Author:** Sivert Gullberg Hansen\
**Keywords:** voice chat, latency, WebRTC, WebTransport, Opus, jitter buffer\
**Status:** the comparison with other apps in Section 6.4 is still dummy data

## Abstract

Gryt's voice has always gone over WebRTC, like almost every voice app in a browser.
This post asks where the delay in that path comes from, and whether a connection of
our own does better. We measured it with a beep test: a 1 kHz tone replaces the
microphone, and the listener times when each beep arrives. Part of the delay was ours
and has been fixed. The rest sat inside Chrome, in its jitter buffer and in getting
audio into and out of WebRTC. So we built our own connection, with Opus in 2.5 ms
frames, WebTransport datagrams and our own jitter buffer, plus a small engine in C on
the phone and the Mac. Mic to ear, it was 15 to 27 ms faster in all three setups we
tested. Over the internet, counting only from the beep leaving to it being heard, it
went from 79 ms to 52. A comparison with Discord, TeamSpeak, Mumble, Zoom and Google
Meet is planned, and its charts hold dummy numbers until it's done.

It's experimental, but you can try it today. Beta builds use it by default, and the
stable app has a switch for it under Settings, General, Advanced. The voice server
has to offer it too, which it does from version 1.0.76. On an older one the switch
does nothing, and the call stays on WebRTC.

### Abbreviations

- **ICE, DTLS, RTP:** the protocols WebRTC uses to find a route, encrypt and carry media
- **Jitter buffer:** a short queue on the receiving side that evens out packets arriving unevenly
- **Opus:** the audio codec almost every voice app uses [3]
- **p95:** the 95th percentile, the delay 95 out of 100 measurements stay under
- **QUIC:** the transport under WebTransport and HTTP/3 [5]
- **SFU:** the voice server, which forwards each person's audio to everyone else in the room

## 1 Introduction

My girlfriend and I use Gryt every day, on cables to the same router, and the voice
still felt a bit slow sometimes. On a LAN the distance is a few metres of cable, so
a delay you can hear has to come from software, and software can be changed.

This post answers three questions:

1. **Where does the time go** in Gryt's WebRTC voice path?
2. **How much faster** is a voice connection of our own, from mic to ear?
3. **How does Gryt compare** with the voice apps people already use?

Section 2 covers how voice usually travels in a browser. Section 3 describes how we
measured. Section 4 answers the first question, Section 5 describes what we built,
and Section 6 answers the second and third. Section 7 covers sound quality, cost,
security and what doesn't work yet, and Section 8 sums up.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Voice over WebRTC

A browser call usually works like this. The microphone feeds a Web Audio graph,
where effects such as noise gates and compressors run. WebRTC [1] then encodes the
audio with Opus, sends it as RTP inside DTLS over a route ICE has found, and hands it
to the voice server. On the other side, Chrome's jitter buffer holds packets for a
moment so they can be played back evenly, decodes them, and stretches or squeezes the
audio when it falls behind.

All of that is built into the browser. An app can ask for things, like a smaller
frame size, but it can't replace the parts.

### 2.2 Opus

Opus [3] encodes audio in frames, and a frame can't be sent until it's full. So the
frame size is a delay paid on every packet. WebRTC uses 20 ms frames by default.
Opus itself goes down to 2.5 ms, and encoding a frame costs a fraction of a
millisecond. The codec was never the slow part.

### 2.3 WebTransport

WebTransport [4] gives a web page a connection to a server over QUIC [5]. It can
send datagrams [6]: small packets that are never resent. For voice that's what you
want, because a packet that arrives late is no use to anyone.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 The beep test

The sender's microphone is swapped for a beep, a 1 kHz tone every three seconds. The
listener notes when it hears each one. When both run on the same machine they share
a clock, so there's nothing to sync. We also listen in at a few points along the
way, so the total can be split into stages. Each run is a few minutes long, and all
numbers are medians. Appendix A has the details.

Spotting a beep means listening for a moment first, so the measuring itself costs 20
to 40 ms. Both connections pay that, so the difference between them is real while
the totals are a bit high. Where it matters, we also report the time from the beep
leaving the sender to it being heard, which leaves that cost out.

### 3.2 Setups

**Table 1:** The setups we measured. Every setup was run once on each connection.

| Setup | Clients | Voice server | Network |
|---|---|---|---|
| One machine | Both on one Mac | On the same Mac | None |
| LAN | Both on one Mac | Another box on the LAN | Home LAN |
| Internet | Both on one Mac | Our server, reached through our VPS | Out to the internet and back |

### 3.3 Single parts

Some parts were timed alone. The effects were each run through an
OfflineAudioContext and timed to the sample. The Mac's sound system was timed out and
back in through a virtual cable, so only the software path counts.

### 3.4 Comparison with other apps

The beep test only works where we control both ends, which rules out other apps.
For those we'll film the test with a phone instead. A clap is recorded together with
the sound coming out of the other PC, and the gap between the two is timed from the
video's audio track, 20 to 30 claps per app. We'll do that on a LAN for apps that can
be self-hosted, and over the internet for all of them. Appendix B has the plan. This
part hasn't been done yet.

## 4 Where the time went

On one machine, with nothing in between, Gryt's WebRTC path took about 50 to 64 ms
from mic to speaker. Table 2 lists what we found. Some of it was ours to fix.

**Table 2:** What added delay in Gryt's WebRTC path, and what happened to it.

| What | Cost | Status |
|---|---|---|
| eSports mode still ran the compressor, limiter and clipper | 6 + 6 + 4 ms | Fixed |
| eSports mode asked for 10 ms frames in the wrong half of the handshake, so Chrome kept 20 | 10 ms | Fixed |
| Every reconnect reused the audio track and left the call a bit slower | Grew until you left voice | Fixed |
| Turning all processing off didn't take it out of the chain | Varies | Fixed |
| Chrome's jitter buffer | About 30 ms, whatever we asked for | Inside Chrome |
| Getting audio into and out of WebRTC | About 12 ms | Inside Chrome |

The reconnect bug was the "it gets laggier the longer we talk" we'd noticed at home.

## 5 What we built

We kept Opus and replaced everything around it:

- **Opus in WebAssembly**, built from the official libopus source [7], set to its
  lowest-delay mode with 2.5 ms frames. Figure 2 shows what that saves on every packet.
- **An AudioWorklet** [8] that encodes and decodes on the audio thread. The gate,
  volume, auto gain, compressor and limiter are plain code in there, with no lookahead.
- **WebTransport** to the voice server, with each packet sent as a datagram. Nothing
  gets resent.
- **Our own jitter buffer**, sized from the jitter it actually sees. After a burst it
  drains back down instead of staying big.
- **The voice server** forwards each packet to everyone else in the room and puts
  the sender's id in front. It takes that id from the session, never from the packet.

In a browser without WebTransport, or on a network that blocks the UDP it needs, the
same packets go over a normal WebSocket to the voice server instead. It's slower on a
bad network, but you're still in the same call as everyone else.

On the phone and in the Mac desktop app it goes one step further. A small engine in
C, built on miniaudio [9], does the capture, the Opus and the playback itself, and
talks to the voice server over plain UDP. Figure 4 shows what that does to the Mac's
sound system alone. Video and screen sharing stay on WebRTC everywhere. Only voice

### 6.3 Does it still work

We checked the boring parts too. Mute let no beeps through, and push to talk only let
them through while the key was held. Switching microphones mid-call kept the voice
going. Killing the voice server mid-call brought both people back on the new
connection about 8 seconds later, with no extra delay afterwards.

## 7 Discussion

### 7.1 How it sounds, and what it costs

It sends 192 kbps, or 256 when your microphone really is stereo. Gryt's WebRTC voice
was 64 kbps mono by default. Table 3 has the listening test we ran with a song. A
recording of a real call on the new connection came out at the original's level and
very clear. A mono microphone gains nothing from the stereo part.

**Table 3:** Listening test with a song, by bitrate.

| Bitrate | How it sounded |
|---|---|
| 96 kbps | The lowest that held up |
| 128 kbps | Great |
| 192 kbps | The same as the original |

The catch is data. To keep the delay down it sends 400 small packets a second, and
the headers on those cost about 190 kbps by themselves. So at the default you upload
about 380 kbps, which is a lot more than WebRTC used. That's why there's a voice
quality setting from 96 to 192 kbps, on the desktop under Advanced and on the phone
under Voice & video. Turn it down on phone data or weak Wi-Fi. If a channel has a max
bitrate, you send whichever is lower, but never below 96.

For a while your voice also went out over WebRTC, so people without the new
connection could still hear you. That meant uploading it twice. With the newest beta
app and server, the WebRTC copy only goes while someone in the call actually needs it.

### 7.2 Is it safe

It's a new public endpoint, so we treated it like one. In the browser, audio is
encrypted with TLS 1.3 [10] as part of QUIC, the same way WebRTC encrypts it with
DTLS. The voice server's certificate is short-lived and pinned: the app gets its
fingerprint over the connection it already trusts, so it can't be swapped in between.
The C engine's UDP is sealed with ChaCha20-Poly1305 [11] under a key that comes over
that same trusted connection, and anything replayed is thrown away.

To get in at all you need a one-time ticket, which the voice server only hands out
after you've joined the room. It's gone after 30 seconds or one use. The voice server
checks the size of every packet, caps how fast one person can send, and never takes
the sender's id from the packet. Still on the list before it's the default: fuzzing
the packet path, and a cap on sessions per IP address.

### 7.3 Limitations

The measurements have limits of their own. Every run so far had both clients on one
Mac, so the LAN and internet setups test the network path but not two different
computers. The totals include 20 to 40 ms of measuring, and each setup was run once.
The comparison in Section 6.4 fixes both, since it measures between two real PCs.

The connection has limits too:

- **Older voice servers.** A voice server older than 1.0.76 doesn't offer the new
  connection, so calls there stay on WebRTC, like before.
- **Android.** The engine runs on the iPhone, in TestFlight builds for now. Android
  phones stay on WebRTC until it's built there too.
- **Mixed calls, one way.** Someone on an older app version can't hear people on the
  phone or the Mac engine, because those don't send a WebRTC copy at all.
- **The Mac App Store version** can't ship the C engine, so it uses the browser
  version of the new connection.
- **Lost packets.** A packet that doesn't arrive is filled in by Opus, which you can
  hear on a really bad connection.
- **Self-hosting.** The voice server needs one more UDP port open, 4433 by default.
  If it's closed, voice goes over the WebSocket instead.

### 7.4 Future work

The plan is to drop WebRTC voice entirely. The new connection becomes the default for
everyone, then the WebRTC microphone goes. Before that, Android needs the engine. We
also want each packet to carry a small copy of the one before it, so one lost packet
can be rebuilt from the next without adding delay. And the comparison in Section 6.4
needs doing.

## 8 Conclusion

**Where does the time go?** Partly in our own code, and that's fixed: effects that
eSports mode left running, a frame size request Chrome ignored, and a reconnect that
made calls slower over time. The rest sat inside Chrome, about 30 ms in its jitter
buffer and about 12 ms getting audio into and out of WebRTC.

**How much faster is our own connection?** 15 to 27 ms mic to ear across the three
setups. Over the internet, without the measuring, 79 ms became 52.

**How does Gryt compare?** We don't know yet. The method is ready, and the numbers
come when the claps are filmed.

If you're on the beta, or you've flipped the switch, you're already using the new
connection, and we'd love to hear how it sounds.

## Appendix A: The beep test

- **Signal:** a 1 kHz tone, one beep every three seconds, in place of the sender's microphone.
- **Clock:** sender and listener run on the same machine, so both read the same clock.
- **Detection:** the listener reports a beep once it has heard enough of the tone to
  be sure. That wait is the 20 to 40 ms of measuring in Section 3.1.
- **Tap points:** the signal is also read at points along the path, which is how the
  stages in Table 2 and Figure 3 were split out.
- **Run length:** a few minutes per setup and connection, reported as the median.

## Appendix B: Plan for the comparison

- **Apps:** Gryt (old and new, browser and Mac app), Discord, TeamSpeak, Mumble, Zoom
  and Google Meet.
- **Setups:** a LAN with the server in the house, for the apps that can be
  self-hosted. The internet for all of them. Default settings and each app's fastest
  settings. Phone to phone as well.
- **Recording:** a phone films the sender's clap and the sound from the listener's
  PC at once, so both end up on one audio track with one clock.
- **Count:** 20 to 30 claps per app and setup.
- **Analysis:** a script finds each clap and its playback in the audio track and
  writes the gaps to a CSV, which the charts in Section 6.4 are drawn from. The videos
  go in this post.
