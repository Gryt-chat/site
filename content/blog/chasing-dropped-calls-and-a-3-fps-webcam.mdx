**Title:** Chasing dropped calls and a 3 fps webcam\
**Date:** 21 September 2026\
**Author:** Sivert Gullberg Hansen\
**Keywords:** WebRTC, bandwidth estimation, keyframes, SFU, WebSocket, Cloudflare tunnel

## Abstract

A webcam in our community sent 1080p at 3 to 7 frames a second on a 74 Mbps upload,
and the app now and then lost its connection to every server at once. We rebuilt
both in a test bed: a throwaway server and SFU with two headless Chrome guests and
a relay that could cap the rate, plus a harness that held calls and connections up
for four hours. The webcam came down to four bugs. The SFU asked for a keyframe
every two seconds and never sent Chrome a round-trip time, the microphone was
allowed 510 kbps, the app swapped the camera for itself four times a second, and a
camera turned off and on took the screen share's slot. Through a 700 kbps cap the
camera went from 4 and 8 fps to 29 and 30 fps once those were fixed. The drops had
four causes: Cloudflare restarting its proxy, a reconnect that switched video off,
our own releases, and a router that drops IPv6 every 5 hours 8 minutes. The fixes
are in SFU 1.0.69 and apps 1.11.33 to 1.11.35. A few things are still open, listed
in Section 5.2.

## 1 Introduction

Someone in our community had a webcam that wouldn't behave. It was sending 1080p at
3 to 7 frames a second, and Gryt's video debug overlay blamed bandwidth. Their upload
was 74 Mbps, and Discord had no trouble with the same webcam.

Around the same time, the app would now and then lose its connection to every server
at once. Nothing in the logs said why.

Neither turned out to be one bug. This post answers two questions:

1. **Why did the camera get so little bandwidth** on a line that fast?
2. **Why did connections drop**, and which drops can Gryt do something about?

## 2 Background

The overlay's Avail out number is Chrome's own estimate of how much it can safely
send right now. On the bad sessions it sat at about 559 kbps, and a 1080p camera
doesn't fit in that. So the question was why Chrome's estimate stayed that low.

Our SFU is the server that passes everyone's audio and video along. Chrome builds
its estimate partly from feedback the SFU sends back. The first guess was that the
SFU never sent that feedback. It turned out it sends about ten feedback packets a
second, so the cause was somewhere else.

A keyframe is a whole picture. The frames between keyframes only carry what changed,
so a keyframe is a lot bigger. At 559 kbps, a 1080p keyframe takes seconds to send.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 The video test bed

I ran a throwaway server and SFU, with two guests in a voice channel, each in
headless Chrome. The camera was a 1080p video file with noise in it, noisier than a
real camera would be. Between the camera and the SFU sat a relay. It could add delay,
jitter and packet loss, cap the rate, and count what came back. Both guests read
Chrome's stats once a second.

To reproduce the stuck estimate, I capped the camera's upload at 700 kbps for the
first 20 seconds, with about 60 ms of round-trip time, then lifted the cap. As a
control, Chrome also sent straight to Chrome through the same 700 kbps cap, without
Gryt in between.

### 3.2 The soak harness

For the drops I wrote a harness [5] that keeps calls and connections up for hours
and writes down everything that happens to them. It runs headless browsers with a
fake camera and microphone, sitting in a call. Next to them are probes: connections
to the server and the SFU, from Node and from a browser page, and a health check
every 10 seconds. It logs all of it with timestamps. Afterwards, each drop gets lined
up with what the server, the SFU and the Cloudflare tunnel logged in the same minute.

The probes went three ways at once: through a Cloudflare tunnel to a test server,
straight to the same server over the local network, and to a server on the same
machine as the harness. The calls ran on that last one. The main run lasted four
hours.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Too many keyframes, and no round-trip time

The SFU asked every camera and screen share for a keyframe every two seconds, on a
timer of its own. In a two-minute test call the camera got 60 of those requests, and
the person watching had asked for one. The stats from the webcam we were chasing fit
that too: 21 requests in 238 frames, or one every 1.6 to 3.8 seconds.

The SFU also reports back to the sender on what arrived. Those reports never had a
round-trip time in them, because the SFU never read the sender's own reports. So for
the whole call, Chrome got no round-trip time from us at all.

Table 1 shows the capped test from Section 3.1 with and without the fixes.

**Table 1:** Capped at 700 kbps for 20 seconds, then uncapped.

| SFU | Runs that got stuck | Estimate after the cap | Camera |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old | 2 of 3 | Stayed at exactly 548 kbps for the rest of the call | 30 kbps floor, 4 to 5 fps at 480x270, nearly every frame a keyframe |
| Either fix, or both | None | Passed 2 Mbps within two seconds | Up to 2.5 Mbps, 30 fps, 1080p |

The SFU now only passes on the keyframe requests from people watching, and its
reports carry a round-trip time [1]. Dropping the timer didn't slow down people who
join in the middle of a stream, because their own request gets passed on. Their
first frame came 252 to 303 ms after connecting, against 217 to 430 ms with the
timer. Both changes shipped in SFU 1.0.69.

### 4.2 The microphone got most of a small budget

Unless a channel set its own bitrate, the app allowed the microphone up to 510 kbps,
the most the Opus audio codec can do. Chrome splits its estimate between everything
you send, and it gave the microphone all it was allowed. With a 548 kbps estimate,
the microphone got 510 and the camera 30. The microphone only used 74 to 84 kbps
of it.

The default is 64 kbps now [2]. A channel's own bitrate still wins, and eSports mode
stays at 128.

### 4.3 The camera was swapped for itself four times a second

This one was a plain bug in the app. It re-added your camera to the call over and
over: 485 times in a two-minute call, about four times a second. Each time, it
swapped the camera out for itself.

Every swap cost a keyframe. It also reset how Chrome scales the camera. So on a thin
link, Chrome dropped frames instead of lowering the resolution. In the control from
Section 3.1, the camera ran at 640x360 and 31 fps. Swapping it for itself every
250 ms held it at 1920x1080 and 7 fps.

Table 2 has the app through the same 700 kbps cap, twice without the microphone fix
and this one, and twice with both.

**Table 2:** The app through a 700 kbps cap, two runs each.

| | Frame rate | Resolution | Keyframes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 4 and 8 fps | 480x270 | 55% and 99% |
| After | 29 and 30 fps | 640x360 to 960x540 | 2% |

That last 2% came from the SFU's two-second timer. On a good connection, the camera
now starts at a lower resolution and Chrome raises it from there. It used to go out
at 1080p straight away, because the swaps kept Chrome from scaling it. Both fixes
are in app 1.11.33 [2].

### 4.4 Turning the camera off and on broke screen sharing

This turned up while I was testing something else. If you turned your camera on, off
and on again, then shared your screen, everyone else was told your screen wasn't
coming through. If you shared first and then turned the camera off and on, the camera
never came back.

The SFU gives each connection four slots to receive on: microphone, camera, screen
and screen audio. Turning the camera off took it out of its slot. And a slot that's
been used once can't be used again. So when the camera came back, it took the next
free video slot. That was the screen's, so a screen share had nowhere to go.

Now the camera pauses in its slot and picks up there again [3]. That's in 1.11.33 too.

### 4.5 The drops

Table 3 has the four-hour run from Section 3.2.

**Table 3:** Drops in the four-hour run, by path.

| Path | Drops |
|---|---|
| Through the Cloudflare tunnel | 6 |
| Straight to the server over the local network | 3 |
| On the harness's own machine | 0 |

The calls on the harness's machine lost no audio packets. The drops came from four
causes and two I can't fully explain.

**Cloudflare restarting its proxy ended the call.** Twice, Cloudflare closed an SFU
connection with code 1001, "CloudFlare WebSocket proxy restarting". A probe just
reconnected, in two or three seconds. A call didn't. The app read a clean 1001 as the
call ending on purpose, so it never reconnected. Meanwhile the SFU dropped the person
and told the server, and eleven seconds later the server took them out of voice. When
a connection just died, with no close code, the app did reconnect, and the call was
back in 1.6 seconds. Our SFU never closes a connection with 1001 itself, so a 1001
always means something in between went away. Now only a normal close ends the call.
A 1001 reconnects like any other drop, and the call is back in 1.6 to 2 seconds
without anyone leaving it. The fix is in app 1.11.34 [6].

**A reconnected call came back with audio only.** When the app reconnected a call on
its own, every camera and screen share had been switched off, and everyone's tiles
waited for video that never came. The app turned your camera off whenever the call
wasn't connected, and a call also counts as not connected for the second or two a
reconnect takes. Now the camera and screen share only stop once the call has really
ended. With the fix, both test guests saw both videos again 3.7 seconds after the
drop. It's fixed in 1.11.35 [7].

**Our own releases.** Two of the drops were our own deploys. Connections drop and are
back within 3 to 8 seconds. Our deploys only replace the SFU when nobody's in a call
on it. A call carries on through a server restart, and in a local test the audio and
video never stopped. The video tiles do go blank for about six seconds while the
server connection is down, though.

**My router drops IPv6 every 5 hours 8 minutes.** One drop hit only the probes going
through the tunnel. My router at home drops its IPv6 route on a fixed timer, every
5 hours, 8 minutes and 34 seconds, and puts it back about two and a half seconds
later. Everything in the house talks to Cloudflare over IPv6 when it can, so every
Gryt connection from the house dies at that moment. The tunnel talks to Cloudflare
over IPv4, so it didn't notice, and cloudflared logged nothing. The apps noticed at
their next ping timeout, 16 seconds later, and were back in under 2 seconds. The same
timer lines up with earlier drops in our servers' logs on 16 and 20 September.

**Unexplained.** In one drop, both ends lost the connection within 18 ms of each
other, so it was closed somewhere between them, in Cloudflare or on the tunnel. It
was back in 0.7 seconds, and the app doesn't show a drop that short. The other was my
machine losing its path to the server over the local network, and no real client
connects that way.

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 If you host behind a Cloudflare tunnel

- Keep the app updated. 1.11.34 stops a Cloudflare restart from ending the call, and
  1.11.35 brings cameras and screen shares back after a reconnect. The desktop app
  [updates itself](https://docs.gryt.chat/docs/use/updates).
- Update the server to 1.10.19 and the SFU to 1.0.69. With Docker Compose that's
  `docker compose pull` and then `docker compose up -d`, as
  [the docs](https://docs.gryt.chat/docs/host/docker-compose#upgrading) say [4]. The
  Windows and Linux 1.10.19 bundles come with the new SFU, and if you host from the
  desktop app, 1.11.33 has both.
- If UDP isn't reliable where cloudflared runs, think about running it over HTTP/2.
  Twice in two nights our whole tunnel went down, for 141 and then 103 seconds. Both
  times our internet connection had lost UDP but still carried HTTPS. Our connectors
  were on QUIC, which runs over UDP, and they didn't switch to HTTP/2 by themselves
  either time. HTTP/2 runs over TCP, so it would probably have stayed up. Set
  `TUNNEL_TRANSPORT_PROTOCOL=http2` in cloudflared's environment, or pass
  `--protocol http2`. Ours have run that way since 21 September, so it's too early to
  say it fixed it.
- If all your connections from one place drop at the same moment every few hours,
  check whether your router is dropping IPv6. Gryt can't keep those connections,
  because the network under them has gone.

### 5.2 Limitations and what's still open

- The calls ran on the harness's own machine. Calls through the tunnel haven't had
  their four hours yet, because the harness had no invite for the test server.
- Video tiles go blank for about six seconds while the server connection is down,
  even though the call carries on.
- The server logs nothing when it stops for a release, so a release looks like a
  network drop in the logs.
- Whether a camera should start at a higher resolution on a good connection.

## 6 Conclusion

**Why did the camera get so little bandwidth?** Chrome's estimate got stuck low
because the SFU never sent it a round-trip time and kept asking for keyframes. The
microphone then took 510 kbps of what was left, and the app swapping the camera for
itself four times a second kept Chrome from lowering the resolution instead of the
frame rate. With all of that fixed, the capped camera went from 4 and 8 fps to 29
and 30.

**Why did connections drop?** Cloudflare restarting its proxy, our own releases, and
a router dropping IPv6 on a timer. Gryt can't prevent those. What it can do is
recover: a 1001 now reconnects instead of ending the call, and video comes back after
a reconnect.
